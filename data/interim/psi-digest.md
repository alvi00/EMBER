Fundamentally, the unique buoyancy-free environment offered by microgravity affords simple aerodynamic flow fields to acquire diffusion flame data of high fidelity, with the prospect of understanding unit thermochemical processes as well as quantifying the associated physico-chemical parameters.

### PSI-69 | FLEX | Flame Extinguishment Experiment
platform=International Space Station (ISS) | hw=Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR)-Fluids and Combustion Facility (FCF), Multi-user Droplet Combustion Appartatus (MDCA) | center=Glenn Research Center (GRC) | agency=National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) | sub=Spacecraft Fire Safety | 08-01-2007–07-31-2016 | doi=10.60555/mbq8-0451 | pubs=51 | related=
OBJ: The FLEX (Flame Extinguishment Experiment) investigation aims to map the flammability limits of liquid fuels in microgravity environments, measure the effectiveness of various gaseous fire suppressants such as CO2, He, and SF6, and develop reliable predictive models for combustion limits under reduced gravity conditions. The mission seeks to validate and improve simplified models by incorporating detailed chemical reactions, heat transfer, and material movement, using both space-based data and extensive ground-based tests. Key flight experiment goals include obtaining high-resolution measurements of droplet burning rates, flame extinction behavior, heat radiation, soot levels, and temperature profiles, while systematically analyzing the effects of different ambient conditions and suppressant concentrations. These investigations are essential for advancing the fundamental understanding of combustion processes in microgravity and enhancing fire safety protocols for future space missions.
APP: The FLEX experiments involve igniting and observing the burning behavior of relatively large droplets. A primary objective of the experiments is to determine the Limiting Oxygen Index (LOI) as a function of ambient pressure and determine how the presence of a suppressant influences the LOI . The basic outline of the procedure to construct this diagram is as follows: 1. Select the test pressure (0.5 − 1.5 atm) for the LOI tests. 2. Select a smaller droplet size (≈ 3 mm) 3. Select an ambient oxygen mole fraction that, based on ground-based testing, will support droplets burning to completion (as opposed to extinguishing at a finite droplet size). 4. Perform a droplet combustion test. 5. Increase the droplet size or repeat the test (if finite extinction diameter at flame extinction observed). 6. Continue steps 4 and 5 until flame exists for only a short time after ignition (radiative extinction). 7. Continue steps 4 thru 6 at a decreased ambient oxygen mole fraction. 8. Continue step 7 to an ambient oxygen mole fraction where the droplet does not ignite. There were ambient conditions where the extinction droplet sizes at all ambient oxygen mole fractions were too small to either be observed or measured with the available diagnostics. In this case, the determination of the LOI is similar, but more iterative. Instead of observing flame extinction, however, the procedure involved an iterative sequence of tests (burn to completion followed by a no ignition test) with progressively smaller increments in ambient oxygen mole fraction to determine the LOI . For the slow flow tests (in the flight experiment), the configuration of the flight hardware did not allow for adequate diagnostics over the entire test for a droplet in a uniform, steady convective flow. In these tests, the procedure will be to duplicate tests above which yield finite-sized extinction droplet diameters. Near the end of the tests, prior to flame extinction, the droplet will be subjected to a small, sub-buoyant, convective flow (imparted by translating the droplet at a slow, uniform speed). The goal of the experiment is then to see how the presence of this flow influences the extinction process. Note, a large number of the tests involve free droplets (as opposed to fiber-supported droplets) and a number of these tests will have droplets with a slow drift velocity. Data gathered from these tests to examine the influences of a small, sub-buoyant flow on both the burning and extinction process.
HYP: The primary hypothesis of the FLEX mission is that microgravity conditions fundamentally alter the combustion and extinction characteristics of liquid fuel droplets due to changes in species and energy transport dynamics, radiative heat loss, and chemical kinetics. Specifically, the hypothesis posits that the lack of buoyant convection in microgravity will lead to significant differences in the flammability limits and suppression efficacy of various fire suppressant agents compared to normal gravity conditions. This hypothesis underpins the design of the FLEX experiments, which aim to map flammability boundaries, quantify suppressant efficacy, and develop predictive models to improve fire safety in space environments.
IMP: The research impacts and Earth benefits of the FLEX mission are significant and multifaceted. By enhancing our understanding of microgravity combustion, the mission directly contributes to improved fire safety protocols for future space missions, ensuring the safety of crew members in environments where traditional fire suppression methods may be ineffective. On Earth, the insights gained from these experiments have the potential to advance fire safety in confined and reduced-gravity environments, such as submarines or high-altitude aircraft. The mission's findings on the effectiveness of various fire suppressants, including CO2, He, and SF6, in microgravity can lead to the development of more efficient and less harmful fire suppression systems, reducing the risk of toxic byproducts during fire events. Additionally, the study of ''cool flames'' and low-temperature combustion processes observed in microgravity can inform the design of advanced combustion engines, leading to improved fuel efficiency and reduced emissions in automotive and industrial applications. Overall, the FLEX mission's contributions to combustion science have the potential to enhance both space exploration safety and terrestrial fire safety technologies.

### PSI-26 | BASS | Burning and Suppression of Solids
platform=International Space Station (ISS) | hw=Microgravity Science Glovebox | center=Glenn Research Center (GRC) | agency=National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) | sub=Spacecraft Fire Safety | 10-01-2009–06-30-2013 | doi=10.60555/58h4-dy80 | pubs=10 | related=PSI-107,PSI-25
OBJ: The two main objectives for BASS are to: 1.) Begin to bridge the gap between the normal gravity NASA-STD-6001 Test#1 method, ground-based microgravity tests, and actual material flammability in microgravity. The relation between these can be investigated by observing flames burning for the longer times available in flight. Practical, realistic (thicker) fuels in typical geometries will be examined, including wake flames which have characteristics of a fire burning behind another object (for example, a fire burning in tight area which may not be easily accessible). 2.) Assess the effectiveness of an inert, gaseous extinguishing agent (similar to that used on ISS) in putting out flames over different materials, geometries, preheating, and flow.
APP: The experiment was conducted aboard the International Space Station (ISS) inside the Microgravity Glove Box (MSG). Custom-made fabric samples consisting of 75% cotton and 25% fiberglass (by mass) measuring were used as a fuel source as well as Nomex fabric and Ultem. A Kanthal wire (29-gage)igniter was used to initiate combustion at one end of the fabric. Airflow was controlled using a variable speed fan, with airflow speeds reaching as high as 40 cm/s. The airflow was directed along the fuel surface in either the opposed or concurrent direction. This investigation explored two primary air flow configurations; concurrent-flow and opposed-flow. With concurrent-flow air flows in the same direction as the flame spread and in opposed-flow air flows in the opposite direction of flame spread. Flames were observed in the top and edge profiles and data was collected on flame size, shape, color, spread rate, and potential flame quenching. The samples were placed in a small flow duct (76 mm x 76 mm cross section) that allowed for airflow across the fabric. Other sample shapes were utilized as well including spheres and cylinders.
HYP: BASS experiments were based on the hypothesis that materials in microgravity, with adequate ventilation, burn as well if not better than the same material in normal gravity with other conditions being identical (pressure, oxygen concentration, temperature, etc.) To test this hypothesis with any but the thinnest materials requires extended microgravity time – longer than available in drop towers or parabolic aircraft.
IMP: Current NASA spacecraft materials selection is based on a standard test method (NASA–STD–6001 Test 1) that segregates material based on 1-g behavior without consideration of low gravity effects. A critical element of this understanding is the radiative heat emission from the flame. These results are used in first order models and predictions of heat release in spacecraft fires and as a means to extend heat release data from tests like the NASA cone calorimeter test (NASA–STD–6001 Test 2) to a performance-based material selection process. Using nitrogen as a flame suppressant in microgravity provides a direct link to current and planned extinguishment techniques. BASS results provide essential guidance to ground-based microgravity combustion research efforts. Detailed combustion models are validated using the simpler flow environment afforded by tests in microgravity. Once validated, they can be used to build more complex combustion models needed to capture the important details of flames burning in normal gravity. These models have wide applicability to the general understanding of many terrestrial combustion problems and will guide novel spacecraft design and ensure the safety of future crewed missions.

### PSI-47 | DAFT / DAFT-2 | Dust and Aerosol Measurement Feasibility Test / Dust and Aerosol Measurement Feasibility Test-2
platform=International Space Station (ISS) | hw=TSI P-Trak | center=Glenn Research Center (GRC) | agency=National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) | sub=Spacecraft Fire Safety | 03-01-2003–06-01-2009 | doi=10.60555/ztgj-d202 | pubs=2 | related=PSI-102
OBJ: The focus of DAFT / DAFT-2 was to mitigate the risk for the Smoke Aerosol Measurement Experiment (SAME). SAME relied on a commercially available condensate nuclei counter, the TSI P-Trak, to quantify smoke aerosols in a microgravity environment, however there were concerns that th P-Trak would not be reliable in microgravity as it relies on gravity to recycle isopropyl alcohol used in the measurement process. DAFT / DAFT-2 tests that a modified P-Trak particulate counter would be accurate in microgravity. Summarized objectives include: 1) Demonstrate the Functionality of the Modified TSI P-Trak in Microgravity 2) Compare the TSI P-Trak Performance against the DustTrak control 3) Create and Analyze Aerosol Standards in Microgravity 4) Ensure the reliability of future aerosol experiments
APP: The experimental approach consists of the following components: 1) Modification of Instrumentation: TSI P-Trak was modified for use in microgravity by redesigning the condenser tube to use capillary forces for recycling isopropyl alcohol and removing the mercury switch responsible for detecting instrument orientation. 2) Aerosol Generation: Arizona Red Dust (ARD) was chosen as an aerosol standard due to its ease of dispersion. The ARD was aerosolized with GN2 to create particle samples. The aerosol was contained within 15-liter Mylar bags. 3) On-Orbit Testing: The modified P-Trak gathered data from counting aerosol particles and these data were compared to the DustTrak CNC specifically designed to work in microgravity.
HYP: The TSI P-Trak operates by condensing isopropanol around aerosol particulates that are subsequently laser counted. It was hypothesized that the P-Trak would not work in microgravity due to two potential failure modes that exist. 1) the system would clog or become obscured by isopropanol, and 2) the internal air-flow dynamics of the optics chamber of the device will cause an over counting of particulates. The TSI P-Trak was modified for microgravity use via using capillary action to recycle the isopropanol. Therefore, there are two basic hypotheses: 1) that the P-Trak, as modified for use in SAME, would function normally in microgravity. 2) that the aerosol created and filtered per the experiment design would be within the measurement capabilities of the test devices.
IMP: Extended duration space flight missions with larger vessels will require fire detection systems with increased reliability and sensitivity. This research will impact the development of novel fire and smoke detection systems for the International Space Station and future spacecrafts. Additionally, the successful adaptation of the TSI P-Trak for microgravity can inform the design of instruments for use in other challenging Earth environments, such as submarines or underwater laboratories where accurate detection of smoke can save lives.
