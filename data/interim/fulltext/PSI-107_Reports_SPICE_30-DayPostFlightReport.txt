I.  
Introduction 
 
A. 
Hypothesis 
The hypothesis of the SPICE investigation is that smoke point behavior 
will be observed for co-flowing flames and that it will show 
dependence on nozzle diameter and coflow velocity. 
 
B. 
Objectives of Investigation 
The objective of the SPICE experiment is to find the laminar smoke-point 
properties of nonbuoyant jet diffusion flames (i.e., the properties of the 
largest laminar jet diffusion flames that do not emit soot) for several fuels 
under different nozzle diameter/ co-flow velocity configurations.  
Luminous flame shape measurements would also be made to verify 
models of the flame shapes under co-flow conditions.  
 
C. 
Previous Mission Experience 
 
N/A 
 
D. 
Background/History of Project (Optional) 
 
The SPICE experiment was an outgrowth of the Laminar Soot Processes 
Experiment (LSP) which flew on STS 83, 94, and 107.  SPICE utilized the 
design heritage and modified the hardware from the Enclosed Laminar Flames 
Experiment (ELF) which flew in the Middeck Glovebox on STS 87. 
 
II.  Methods/Research Operations 
 
A. 
Discussion of Method/Protocol 
 
Most tests were conducted by setting the coflow velocity, igniting the flame, 
and adjusting the fuel flow rate. Approximate smoke points were identified in 
the video by the flight crew and simultaneously by the ground support crew in 
Cleveland. Typical burn times were 60 s, and after each two such burns the 
MSG contents were flushed with ISS cabin air.  After the tests the video 
record was analyzed to identify smoke point conditions. The associated 
coflow and fuel flow rates were obtained from digital data recorded in the 
video. The smoke point lengths, LSP, were obtained from the video record 
and are the measured distance from the burner tip to the tip of the luminous 
flame on the flame axis.

List of Preflight, In-flight, Postflight Anomalies 
 
SPICE operated very successfully with the only significant anomaly being the

outlet screen on June 15.

Laminar Smoke Points of  
Microgravity Coflow Diffusion Flames

Laminar smoke points were measured in nonbuoyant laminar jet diffusion flames in air coflow aboard the 
International Space Station as part of the Smoke Points in Coflow Experiment (SPICE). Smoke points were found 
for ethylene, propane, propylene, and mixtures of 50 and 75% propylene by volume in nitrogen. Burner diameters 
were 0.41, 0.76 and 1.6 mm and coflow velocities varied from 5.4 – 65 cm/s. These flames afford good control over 
residence time via variations in dilution, burner diameter, and coflow velocity. Increasing coflow velocity decreases 
luminous flame lengths, but moves smoke points to higher fuel flow rates and longer luminous flame lengths. 
Smaller burners have longer smoke points for coflowing flames. These trends can be explained qualitatively by 
considering total residence time effects. The luminous flame lengths generally correlate with fuel mass flow rate 
divided by stoichiometric mixture fraction, although they increase slightly as air velocity decreases.  
1. Introduction 
Soot formation is an important unresolved problem in combustion science. Soot is estimated to 
be responsible for up to 50,000 premature deaths in the U.S. [1] and is believed to contribute to 
climate change and glacial melting [2]. Radiation from soot in flames allows reading by candle-
light but also contributes to heat loads in engines and the spread of unwanted fires. Smoking 
flames emit carbon monoxide, which is the major cause of death in fires [3].  
The smoke point is the condition (or length) where a flame begins to emit soot. Smoke points 
indicate the sooting propensity of a fuel in diffusion flames. In normal gravity, laminar smoke 
points have been measured for many gaseous and condensed fuels [4–9]. Laminar smoke points 
have been found to correlate with soot volume fractions [10] and radiative loss factions [11] of 
turbulent diffusion flames. Yang et al. [12] found that gas turbine soot emissions correlate very 
well with a threshold sooting index (TSI) based on fuel molecular weight divided by the laminar 
smoke point. Smoke points have been shown to correlate with the smoke release from turbulent 
buoyant flames [13] and have been applied to developing general soot models for computational 
combustion simulations [14] and heat release measurements [15]. 
In a 1988 paper [16], Glassman predicted that smoke points would not occur in nonbuoyant gas-
jet flames. He based this on the assumption that residence time scales with the square root of fuel 
flow rate in normal gravity but is independent of flow rate in microgravity flames. Subsequent 
work [17] observed smoke points of nonbuoyant flames aboard a parabolic aircraft and used 
numerical predictions to show that residence time increases with flame length for nonbuoyant 
flames without co-flow. Urban et al. [18] reported smoke points of microgravity flames observed 
on a space shuttle. Microgravity smoke point measurements are difficult in parabolic aircraft

Attributing smoke point behavior to total residence time helps with qualitative explanations of 
smoke point behavior but to date has not yielded successful quantitative correlations [8]. This 
may be because smoke points arise from a competition between the times available for soot 
formation and soot oxidation. Delichatsios and co-workers suggested that smoke point length is 
proportional to soot formation time [19–20]. Quantitative correlations may also be possible from 
an examination of radiative losses, which have been observed to be 20 – 30% for normal-gravity 
smoke point flames and to increase with adiabatic flame temperature [5]. 
Soot is transported in flames by convection and thermophoresis. By varying the burner diameter, 
coflow velocity, and fuel dilution, flame shape and residence times in the soot formation and 
soot oxidation regions can be changed. A decrease in coflow velocity will generally increase 
entrainment of flow towards the flame axis. 
Kent and Wagner [21] found that smoke burn out in the oxidative region of the flame ceases 
when the tip of a buoyant diffusion flame cools to approximately 1300 K. Urban et al. [22] found 
that the tip of a nonbuoyant (microgravity) flame reaches 1000 K at its smoke point. In buoyant 
flames this decrease in temperature is attributed primarily to heat conduction, while in 
nonbuoyant flames it is attributed largely to radiative quenching [23]. The radiative quenching 
effect causes a visible tip opening in nonbuoyant smoke points of flames of relatively long 
residence time [22]. 
A recent 2.5 s drop tower study by Jeon et al. [24] found that for small coflow velocities the soot 
volume fraction increases with coflow velocity. This was attributed to an increase in 
temperature. Lin and Faeth [7,25] studied the effects of coflow velocity on weakly-buoyant 
flames near the smoke point. They observed flames in normal gravity, but at low pressures to 
reduce the effects of gravity. They found that increasing the coflow velocity reduced soot 
volume fractions and increased the smoke point length. This was attributed to decreased flame 
residence time and modified soot pathlines. Refs. [7 and 24] both showed that luminous flame 
lengths decrease with increasing coflow velocity. Their burner diameters, 7 and 10 mm, 
respectively, were much larger than those considered here. 
Faeth and co-workers [25–27] applied the flame shape analyses of Spalding [28] and 
Mahalingam et al. [29] to nonbuoyant gas-jet flames to obtain

2. Experimental 
The Smoke Points in Coflow Experiment (SPICE) module was brought to the International 
Space Station (ISS) in 2008. It was operated in the Microgravity Science Glovebox (MSG) by 
various crew members in 2009. Smoke points were attempted for all fuels for fuel jet diameters 
of 0.41, 0.76, and 1.6 mm. The conditions for which smoke points were found are summarized in 
Table 1. In some cases (e.g., tests involving ethylene) the fuel supply was depleted before 
desired smoke points could be found, as the supply was limited to two 75 cc bottles of each fuel 
at gage pressures of 760 – 1440 kPa, depending on the fuel. 
 The SPICE module contains a rectangular duct that has an approximately 76 mm square cross 
section with rounded corners. A DC fan supplies coflowing air from the glovebox contents. The 
air passes through a honeycomb and screen to reduce swirl and then enters the duct. The air and 
combustion products leave the duct through a perforated copper plate followed by a copper 
screen. The screen is 50 mm upstream of the burner discharge and the plate is 120 mm 
downstream. Fan voltage is varied to control the coflow velocity, which is measured with a hot-
wire anemometer between the inlet honeycomb and screen. This anemometer was calibrated in 
normal gravity using a hot-wire anemometer 36 mm downstream of the duct entrance. Fuel flow 
rate was adjusted manually and controlled with a mass flow meter. The flames were ignited with 
a hot-wire ignitor. Video was recorded with an analog color video camera and downlinked in real 
time. Still images were recorded with a 12 bit Nikon™ D100 single-lens reflex color digital still 
camera (with 3008 × 2000 pixels) with a 60 mm lens. 
Most of the tests were conducted by setting the coflow velocity, igniting the flame, and adjusting 
the fuel flow rate. Approximate smoke points were identified in the video by the flight crew and 
simultaneously by the ground support crew in Cleveland. Typical burn times were 60 s, and after 
each two such burns the MSG contents were flushed with ISS cabin air.  
After the tests the video record was analyzed to identify smoke point conditions. The associated 
coflow and fuel flow rates were obtained from digital data recorded in the video. The smoke 
point lengths, LSP, were obtained from the video record and are the measured distance from the 
burner tip to the tip of the luminous flame on the flame axis. The luminous flame tips were not 
sharply defined in most images, but rather involved a transition from bright yellow to black 
across an axial distance of 2 – 5 mm. Flame height was thus measured to the point where the 
centerline grayscale intensity changed most rapidly between these yellow and black regions. This 
helped reduce the sensitivity of measured flame height to camera exposure settings. 
Smoke points were obtained for five fuels, as summarized in Table 1. Smoke points were

3. Results and Discussion 
Some smoke points, propane and ethylene in particular, were identified by the onset of gradual 
(rather than sharp) dimming and reddening of the luminous flame tip in the downstream 
direction. Rounding of the tip also was associated with the smoke point. For conditions 
sufficiently above the smoke point, quenched soot could be seen leaving the flame. The brightest 
flames did not always display significant reddening or darkening near their tips, except well 
above the smoke point. This occurred for the pure propylene and propylene mixtures, and the 
smoke point was identified by the rapid transition to an open-tipped, soot emitting flame.  
The flames generally reached steady conditions within 5 s of ignition or a change in flow rate of 
fuel or air. Flames of propylene and propylene mixtures were generally more luminous than 
those of methane, ethylene, and propane, attributed to their higher propensity to form soot. The 
smoke point transition was more abrupt in the propylene-based fuels than for propane or 
ethylene. Periodic flame motion was observed in some flames, especially those with high coflow, 
long length, and low fuel velocity, and is attributed to non-uniform coflow. This effect was small 
and did not prevent clear measurement of flame length. 
Figure 1 shows images for representative flames near their smoke points. Two sequences are 
shown, and in both the smoke point occurs between the third and fourth flames. In the top 
sequence, coflow velocity decreases from left to right at constant fuel flow rate. This increases 
luminous flame length and reduces the smoke point leading to soot emission. The luminous 
length decreases in the fourth image owing to incomplete combustion. In the bottom sequence 
fuel flow rate is increased at constant coflow velocity, increasing flame length as expected. As 
seen in Fig. 1, luminous length is more sensitive to changes in fuel flow rate than to changes in 
coflow velocity.

The present smoke points were found to be repeatable, except for propylene with a 0.41 mm 
burner. Testing on different days yielded different smoke points for this combination. This is 
attributed to a change in the duct exit system, which became partially clogged with soot and 
required a higher fan voltage to obtain a given hot-wire duct air velocity reading. The lower 
smoke points were obtained with a relatively clogged filter and the highest smoke point was 
found after the filter was vacuumed clean. For all of these tests, the flow rate was set based on 
the hot-wire duct velocity reading, however it appears that the flow restriction in the filter 
changed the coflow. Other smoke points collected on these two dates did not exhibit this same 
discrepancy so this coflow quality effect may only affect flames of this smallest burner diameter.

75% propylene < propylene. (This is consistent with unpublished normal gravity results in the 
same apparatus.) The only exception was that for the 0.76 mm burner ethylene has a longer

Smoke points of microgravity gas-jet 
diffusion 
flames 
were 
observed. 
Conditions emphasized small burners 
and effects of coflow velocity. These 
flames allow extensive control of 
residence time, which is important in 
smoke point phenomena. The major

1. An increase in coflow velocity 
increases the smoke point length. 
2. A decrease in burner diameter
