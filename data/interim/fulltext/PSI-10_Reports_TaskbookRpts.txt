Task Description:
NOTE Update August 2020: New grant issued for Flame Design - A Novel Approach to Clean Efficient Diffusion
Flames, with grant number 80NSSC20M0072, which continues to 4/30/2022. NOTE (September 2015): Continuation of
Flame Design - A Novel Approach to Clean Efficient Diffusion Flames (NNX09AO53A). 
The overwhelming majority of practical combustion processes involve non-premixed combustion of hydrocarbons.
These processes must be efficient and the resulting emissions low. For example, soot production, which can adversely
affect efficiency, emissions, and equipment lifetime, must be controlled, and flame extinction must be avoided. Many
methods have been considered to reduce soot, such as increasing strain rates and inert concentrations and reducing
pressures, but these techniques generally weaken the flame, leading to extinction and resulting in substantial performance
penalties. The proposed study considers a novel method of approaching combustion to address these concerns. The
approach is termed “Flame Design” as it attempts to take our fundamental understanding of diffusion flames and, within
the inherent constraints imposed on the problem, design flames that best optimize efficiency and minimize pollutants. 
This effort is a collaborative effort between the PI (Principal Investigator), the Co-Is (Co-Investigators), and staff at
NASA Glenn Research Center. The division of labor has Richard Axelbaum directing normal gravity experiments and
computations at Washington University, David Urban providing guidance for the flight experiments, and Peter
Sunderland directing experiments and numerical simulations at University of Maryland. 
  
Rationale for HRP Directed Research:  
Research Impact/Earth Benefits:
This project has the potential for improving efficiency and reducing pollutant emissions in terrestrial combustors. For
example, it is contributing to novel designs of future coal power plants. 
  
Task Progress:
We have completed Round 1 (normal flames) and Round 2 (inverse flames) of Flame Design testing and are processing
and analyzing the data. We are running Sphdif simulations and publishing the findings. 
Three journal papers were published this year and one other was submitted. One conference paper was published and
presented. Kendyl Waddell defended and published his Ph.D. dissertation. 
Our work on the numerical model has continued. We have run multiple cases with the University of California San
Diego (UCSD) mechanism. We continue to investigate the boundary conditions for radiation and conduction. We
simulated ethylene flames to perform parametric studies in an attempt to find conditions that are favorable to steady state
flames. 
February 2024 Update per PI. See Bibliography for additional information. (Ed., 2/15/24) 
Despite a range of conditions, all flames extinguish at a critical temperature of 1130 K, a fuel-based mass flux of 0.2
g/m2-s for normal flames, and an oxygen-based mass flux of 0.68 g/m2-s for inverse flames. With this information, we
have developed a simple equation to estimate the flame size (i.e., location of peak temperature) at extinction for any
atmospheric-pressure ethylene spherical diffusion flame given only the reactant mass flow rate. Flame growth, which
ultimately leads to radiative extinction if the critical extinction point is reached, is attributed to the natural development
of the diffusion limited system as it approaches steady state and the reduction in the transport properties as the flame
temperature drops due to increasing flame radiation with time (radiation-induced growth). 
Reference: Irace PH, Waddell KA, Constales D, Sunderland PB, Axelbaum RL. "Critical temperature and reactant mass
flux for radiative extinction of ethylene microgravity spherical diffusion flames at 1 bar." Proc. Combust. Inst. 2023 Jan
1;39(2):1905-13. <a target="_blank" href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.proci.2022.07.043">https://</a> 
Despite a narrow range of attainable conditions, flames that exhibit steady-state behavior are observed aboard the
International Space Station (ISS) for up to 870 seconds, even with the constraint of a finite boundary. Steady-state
flames are simulated using the numerical model for over 100,000 seconds. 
Reference: Irace PH, Waddell KA, Constales D, Kim M, Yablonsky G, Sunderland PB, Axelbaum RL. "On the
existence of steady-state gaseous microgravity spherical diffusion flames in the presence of radiation heat loss." Proc.
Combust. Inst. 2023 Jan 1;39(2):1721-9. <a target="_blank"
href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.proci.2022.07.049">https://</a>

Task Progress:
We have completed Round 1 of Flame Design testing and are processing the data. This includes making videos from
HiBMs still images, converting video from the ops camera from ts to mp4 format, plotting the digital data for all tests,
and measuring flame diameters. We have finalized the test matrix for Round 2. 
We are organizing the results of Flame Design Round 1 (flame diameter, fiber temperature, PMTs, radiometers, etc.).
Particular focus has been placed on using two-color ratio and ratio intensity pyrometry to obtain fiber temperature. We
continued to plot and analyze the thermocouple data. 
We are processing HiBMs data to obtain soot volume fraction and are using the Advanced Combustion via Microgravity
Experiments (ACME) images to obtain soot temperature. Fiber temperature is being used to measure flame temperature
at the sooting limit. We have compiled all of our radiative extinction data, excluding extinction temperature. 
We have performed thin-filament pyrometry for several International Space Station (ISS) flames based on color ratios
and based on grayscale intensity. 
We have completed soot temperature measurements of ethylene and methane tri-axial flames using two-color pyrometry.
We performed narrow-angle radiometer measurements of radiant emissions in ethylene tri-axial flames. Soot volume
fraction measurements using color pyrometry and an absolute intensity calibration have been performed. In our research
on double luminous zones, we have made our chemiluminescence images quantitative and have chosen some of them to
be displayed as color contour plots of intensity at specific wavelengths. Thermocouple probing of the double luminous
zones was performed. Work continued on the imaging of excited species. 
An analytical model of steady-state diameters of gas-fed spherical diffusion flames was created. 
Our work on the numerical model has continued. We have run multiple cases with the University of California San
Diego (UCSD) mechanism. We continue to investigate the boundary conditions for radiation and conduction. Because
burner heating can significantly affect the flame diameter and lifetime, we have started to implement a heat balance at
the burner surface to solve for burner temperature and better conserve enthalpy. We simulated ethylene flames to
perform parametric studies in an attempt to find conditions that are favorable to steady state flames. We compared the
simulation results with experimental results. Work is underway to update the globally implicit solver to the
time-splitting method. We modified the SphDiff code to plot the radius of stoichiometric mixture fraction as a function
of time, both for non-reacting and reacting flows. We added 2nd order upwind scheme. We modified the transient term
in the conservation of mass equation so that it captures the density change due to temperature and concentration
changes. We added an automatic temperature initialization scheme from an analytical droplet combustion model.

Many laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) tests were performed. The system was optimized and the
signal/data processing methodology was improved. We obtained calibration data of the relation between atomic ratio
and intensity ratio of major elements (C,H,O,N) from a McKenna flat-flame burner. We reduced the burden on the
transition stage by cutting down the distance from the measured point to the transition stage (for ethylene-air
counter-flow burner). We measured the C/O, H/O, C/H atomic ratios along the center line of ethylene-air counter-flow
diffusion flame. We used the acoustic system (LIBT) to measure the temperature profile on the center line of an
ethylene-air counter-flow diffusion flame. All the results have been compared to the OPPDIF simulation and
Wengdong's previous data, and we have verified that the LIBS measurement is reliable and repeatable. 
At Washington University in the last two months, we have performed experiments to show the PM emissions from the
pure ethylene flame and comparing PM (particulate matter) data of newly bought low pressure impactor [Electrical
Low-Pressure Impactor (ELPI), model ELPI+, Dekati] and Scanning Mobility Particle Spectrometry (SMPS, model
3080, TSI Inc.). A rich ethylene flame was chosen as it produces soot at a uniform rate. A co-flow diffusion flame
burner was used, which comprised of two coaxial channels for the flow of fuel and air, respectively. Particle sampling is
achieved with an N2-diluted sampling probe. The flow of dilution N2 through the annulus gap provides the negative
pressure needed to sample the flue gas as well as cool down and dilute the particle-laden flow. The dilution ratio (DR)
is controlled by N2 flow rate and the physical dimension of the annulus gap, and CO2 is chosen as the tracer gas for
dilution ratio measurement. A high flow rate of dilution N2 (~45 SLPM) is applied and the dilution ratio is controlled to
a range of 30 – 200. The probe samples the whole particle stream while maintaining quasi-isokinetic sampling, and is
expected to cause little bias for particle measurements. The ELPI and SMPS (scanning mobility particle spectrometer)
data show similar trends. The experiments were also conducted to know the efficiency of ELPI with respect to time to
get the correct PSD (particle size distribution) data. It was found that after 2 minutes of measurements, ELPI have to be
cleaned as it creates a virtual mode owing to the partly over filing of ELPI stages. In the future, a new dilution system
will be used and PM data will be compared between SMPS and ELPI at dilution ratio of the order of 10000 using rich
ethylene flames. 
At University of Maryland (UMD), the possible appearance of double luminous zones in normal diffusion flames was
investigated in the literature and in the laboratory. Additional published flame lengths were added to our draft paper on
flame lengths. Oxygen concentrations were measured in the chimney for inverse diffusion flames. A presentation on
double luminous zones was made at ASGSR (American Society for Gravitational & Space Research) in Seattle. 
At UMD we tested IDFs (inverse diffusion flames) with CO2 as diluent in both fuel side and oxidizer side. These did not
have double luminous zones. We also tested IDFs with Ar and He as diluent. These had the most remarkable double
luminous zones we have seen to date. 
At UMD, work continues on improved imaging of species in normal and inverse diffusion flames. Interference filters
have been acquired to allow the imaging of OH*, CH*, C2*, and CO2*. A Matlab script for Abel deconvolution has
been modified for the resulting images. We have imaged our normal and inverse diffusion flames with various optical
filters to identify the source of the blue emissions observed. We also have applied deconvolution to these images. 
Work has progressed on identifying the species responsible for double blue zones in normal and inverse gas jet diffusion
flames. Bandpass filters allow imaging of C2* (515 nm), CO2* (455 nm), CH* (430 nm), and OH* (310 nm). The
images are deconvolved, and corrections for CO2* emissions are made in the images of C2* and CH* emissions. 
WashU and UMD have been working together to begin welding B-type thermocouples (0.0015 in, 38 micron). These
will initially be used to measure temperatures of co-flow diffusion flames at various stoichiometric mixture fraction. 
The injection port of the single droplet generator has been modified to allow for continuous operation with a Hencken
flat flame. Droplet generation in the previous design was unstable when run with a flame. The single droplet generator
will allow for the study of preferential vaporization in nonpremixed flames. We modified the design of the droplet
generator to be able to maintain a steady droplet stream with flame. Drawings were sent to machine shop and parts have
been modified. 
A new student has started at Wash U and he has been learning high-speed videography, PDPA, and manufacturing of
fine-wire thermocouples. We are also developing a new method of making temperature measurements with high-speed
videography. 
The spherical flame code has been demonstrated for the first time by the new team of Ph.D. students. Work at UMD has
emphasized the numerical model. The current graduate students are now able to simulate spherical normal and inverse
diffusion flames. They have implemented an improved method of choosing the initial temperature profile. 
The numerical modeling of spherical diffusion flames has progressed. This indicates that some normal flames of
ethylene burning in air may be able to reach steady state. Furthermore, flames that continue burning with peak
temperatures as low as 1000 K are observed. Previously no flames were predicted with these temperatures below 1100
K. 
We completed the final review of “Science Protocol on Intent to Conduct an International Collaborative Research Effort
Entitled Flame Design,” to support collaborative research with Russian Scientists. 
Employing the principles developed by Long to support ACME (Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments)
research projects, WashU is developing capabilities to measure particle temperatures through direct photography.

The laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) measurement method has been improved at Washington University
in St. Louis. This includes a new oscilloscope setup and preparation for better acoustic monitoring. We have
synchronized the new oscilloscope with the LIBS system. We completed work on noise filtering of the acoustic
measurements. We performed Matlab programming on automated acoustic aided LIBS measurements. We performed
high temperature LIBS tests in a Hencken burner, LIBS tests in a McKenna burner under same equivalence ratio and
different temperature, and acoustic aided LIBS measurements in a McKenna burner with varying equivalence ratio. We
adapted a maximum likelihood estimation method instead of average in LIBS data processing; compared and correlated
the baseline for different element emission lines; and worked on the scalar measurement. We investigated the
compositional effects in laser-induced backward transfer (LIBT) measurement and investigated the screening methods
of LIBT measurement by looking into the statistics. We investigated the simultaneous composition and temperature
measurement using laser induced plasma. We explored the water-gas-shift equilibrium in higher hydrocarbon flames and
investigated the potential of inferring molecular profiles from LIBS measured elemental compositions. We investigated
the water-gas-shift equilibrium in higher hydrocarbons using detailed mechanisms for heptane and toluene. We
developed a plasma model to simulate the sound wave generation as a function of laser energy input and local gas
properties and to optimize the measurements using a CCD based spectrometer. 
Wendong Wu successfully defended his Ph.D. in May, 2017, at Washington University in St. Louis. The collaboration
between Washington University and Shanghai Jiaotong University will continue in the field of laser plasma diagnostics.
Graduate student Pan Du has joined the Flame Design team. 
At the University of Maryland, we continue to examine the effects of dilution on lengths of normal and inverse gas jet
diffusion flames. The method of measuring flame lengths was refined to examine only the blue intensities in the recorded
images. Both normal and inverse flames now correlate together according to the theory of Roper. We have also
characterized the double flame structure visible in some inverse diffusion flames. We imaged the double flames at 310
and 430 nm. We are measuring oxygen concentrations and temperatures in and above the double flames. This will help
to determine if oxygen in this region contributes to the formation of the double luminous zones. A paramagnetic oxygen
analyzer is being used, which has a mole fraction range of 0-1 and an accuracy of 0.001%. 
At the University of Hawaii Manoa, Beei-Huan Chao has retired and resigned from his role as Flame Design
Co-Investigator. His invaluable contributions to Flame Design are greatly appreciated and will be missed going forward.
Prior to his retirement, progress was made on analytical studies of C/O ratio in diffusion flames. We examined extinction
of spherical diffusion flames attached to a burner and revisited reactant leakage of diffusion flames. We revisited
Linan's 1974 paper following the “total enthalpy” concept. 
The research team published two journal papers, four conference papers and abstracts, and one Ph.D. dissertation. 
The flight experiment was supported in various ways. For example, the research team responded to issues raised by
NASA engineers in refining the test matrix and ACME requirements document. The research team also aided staff at

Additional Accomplishments 
W. Wu, G. Yablonsky, R.L. Axelbaum, Observation of Water-Gas Shift Equilibrium in Diffusion Flames, Combustion
and Flame, accepted. The kinetic behavior of the water-gas-shift (WGS) sub-system has been studied computationally
for hydrocarbon diffusion flames in the counterflow geometry. Of specific interest was the spatial domain in which the
reactions in the WGS-complex exhibit characteristics that lead to species concentrations approaching those expected
from equilibrium. Two scenarios were observed for WGS equilibrium: 
(1) Full equilibrium in which every step of the WGS-mechanism is nearly in equilibrium; 
(2) “Apparent” equilibrium, which is achieved via opposing but coherent shifts away from equilibrium for the two key
elementary reactions that constitute the WGS reaction. This interesting phenomenon is caused by the supply of the H
radical, which participates in the forward reaction of one step and the reverse reaction of the other. This new form of
apparent equilibrium differs from well-known “quasi” equilibrium, which is typically caused by the combination of a
fast reversible step and slow irreversible step. 
Parametric domains where found in which these two scenarios are observed. For a given fuel type it was shown that the
two scenarios can be separated by the location of stoichiometry. On the oxidizer side, full equilibrium of the overall
WGS reaction is achieved, while on the fuel side, apparent equilibrium can occur.

stoichiometric lengths of laminar gas jet diffusion flames. These are found to be independent of pressure and burner
diameter for fixed fuel mass flow rate. However, some studies report flame lengths increase when inert is added to the

fuel stream, whereas others report no change. Furthermore, measurements of effects of inert in the ambient gas are
sparse, as are measurements for inverse flames. The analytical model of Roper (CNF 29:219, 1977) accounts for the full
range of reactant dilution and inverse flames, but has only been tested for fuel-air systems. 
This work examines the stoichiometric lengths of normal and inverse steady methane/oxygen/nitrogen jet flames burning
on a 3.0 mm coflowing burner inside a glass chimney. Nitrogen dilution is such that methane mole fractions vary from
0.15 – 1 and oxygen mole fractions vary from 0.18 – 1. The measured flame lengths vary from 3 – 53 mm.
Stoichiometric lengths are measured from visible emissions of the blue flame sheets. Reactant flow rates are measured
with calibrated rotameters. 
The predictions of the Roper model are compared with the measured flame lengths. For normal flames the agreement is
best for a mass diffusivity at 25 C of 17 mm2/s and a temperature of 0.94 times the adiabatic flame temperature. This
contrasts to values used by Roper for methane/air flames of 20 mm2/s and 1500 K, respectively. For the present
conditions the predictions match the measurements with a R2 coefficient of determination of 0.91. 
For fixed fuel flow rate, inert addition to the fuel stream is found to increase flame length, but this effect is negligible for
fuel mole fractions above 0.5. 
A simplified approximation of the Roper model has been obtained for normal methane/nitrogen/oxygen diffusion
flames. This removes the requirement to specify adiabatic flame temperature and stoichiometric mixture fraction. The
simplified model predicts flame length from the methane flow rate and mole fraction and the oxygen mole fraction. 
W. Wu, R.L. Axelbaum, Interpreting Flame Structure by Mixture Fraction Measurement Using Laser-Induced
Breakdown Spectroscopy, Abstract, ASGSR, Cleveland OH (2016). Measuring mixture fractions in a broad range of
combustion systems with a suitable spatial and temporal resolution is critical not only for understanding the flame
behavior, but also for the validation of rapidly developing numerical models. Instead of deriving the mixture fraction
from measured molecular profile, a new approach of obtaining such quantities from atomic ratios measured by laser
induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) is introduced in this study. The atomic ratios for C, N, H, and O (e.g., C/O,
H/O, and N/O) for the LIBS system were first calibrated in an ethylene-air premixed flat flame, where the equivalence
ratio of the pre-mixture was varied from 0.60 to 1.80. Then the atomic ratio distributions along the centerline of an
ethylene-air counter-flow diffusion flame were measured to demonstrate the capability of LIBS to measure atomic ratios
between different elements throughout the entire flame. The elemental mass fractions for C, H, N, O can be thus
obtained from atomic ratio profiles. The mixture fraction distribution was calculated from elemental mass fractions.
Numerical results were compared to the experimental results and showed excellent agreement. The results of this work
demonstrate that the LIBS technique holds promise as a simple tool for measuring atomic ratios and mixture fraction in
diffusion flames.

V.R. Lecoustre, P.B. Sunderland, B.H. Chao, R.L. Axelbaum, Modeled Quenching Limits of Spherical Hydrogen
Diffusion Flames, Proceedings of the Combustion Institute 34 (2013) 887-894. Hydrogen-air diffusion flames were
modeled with an emphasis on kinetic extinction. The flames were one-dimensional spherical laminar diffusion flames
supported by adiabatic porous burners of various diameters. Behavior of normal (H2 flowing into quiescent air) and
inverse (air flowing into quiescent H2) configurations were considered using detailed H2/O2 chemistry and transport
properties with updated light component diffusivities. For the same heat release rate, inverse flames were found to be
smaller and 290 K hotter than normal flames. The weakest normal flame that could be achieved before quenching has an
overall heat release rate of 0.25 W, compared to 1.4 W for the weakest inverse flame. There is extensive leakage of the
ambient reactant for both normal and inverse flames near extinction, which results in a premixed flame regime for
diffusion flames except for the smallest burners with radii on the order of 1 micron. At high flow rates
H+OH(+M)?H2O(+M) contributes over 40% of the net heat release. However at flow rates approaching quenching
limits, H+O2(+M)?HO2(+M) is the elementary reaction with the largest heat release rate. The corresponding author for
this paper was P.B. Sunderland. 
F. Xia, R.L. Axelbaum, Simplifying the complexity of diffusion flames through interpretation in C/O ratio space,
Computers and Mathematics with Applications, 65 (2013) 1625-1632. Understanding the structure of diffusion flames is
often complicated by the dependence of flame structure on the boundary conditions, e.g. composition, temperature and
flow field (e.g., strain rate in a counterflow flame.) In this paper, the local carbon-to-oxygen atom ratio (C/O ratio) is
applied as a variable to interpret the flame and soot zone structures of counterflow diffusion flames from numerical
results with detailed chemical kinetics and transport. Radical pool and soot precursor zones are shown to be clearly
delineated in C/O ratio space. The boundary of these two zones, as well as the flame location, are shown to be
independent of both stoichiometric mixture fraction (Zst) and strain rate when interpreted in C/O space. The kinetic ratio
is used to study the characteristics of key chemical reactions and to identify regions of equilibrium for these reactions.
The results of this paper indicate that the C/O ratio is a valuable variable for interpreting flame structure and soot
precursor chemistry for diffusion flames. The corresponding author for this paper was R.L. Axelbaum. 
F. Xia, G.S. Yablonsky, R.L. Axelbaum, Numerical Study of Flame Structure and Soot Inception Interpreted in
Carbon-to-Oxygen Atom Ratio Space, Proceedings of the Combustion Institute, 34 (2013) 1085-1091. The utility of
interpreting flame results in carbon-to-oxygen atom ratio (C/O ratio) space, as opposed to physical space or mixture
fraction space, is evaluated. Flame and soot zone structures of counterflow diffusion flames have been studied for C2H4
and C3H8 and interpreted in C/O ratio space as a function of stoichiometric mixture fraction (Zst). The
Burke-Schumann results expressed in C/O ratio space demonstrate how a clear and direct understanding of how
structure is affected by Zst can be realized in C/O ratio space because, unlike physical or mixture fraction space, the
flame location is independent of stoichiometric mixture fraction. Numerical results with detailed chemical kinetics also
indicate that C/O ratio space is a fundamental variable in the sense that, for a given fuel, the location of the flame zones
and critical reactions is invariant with Zst and strain rate. Two zones are clearly observed, namely the radical pool and
the soot precursor zone located on the fuel side of the flame. The onset threshold of soot precursors (C6H5 and C6H6)
for the high temperature side of the soot precursor zone is characterized by the depletion of radicals. The role of the
hydrogen radical in flame structure and soot inception is demonstrated by studying its production and consumption
channels in C/O ratio space. Finally, a modified (C/O)* is given to interpret the physical meaning of C/O ratio. The
numerical results in this work indicate and explain the advantages of applying C/O ratio space in the analysis of flame
structure and soot precursor chemistry. The corresponding author for this paper was R.L. Axelbaum.

with water was evaluated. This concept could potentially be utilized to directly generate steam for a variety of
applications, including power generation and enhanced oil recovery. Two water-soluble fuels, ethanol and 1-propanol,

Task Progress:
The following is a summary of the research activities during the period Oct. 2006 - Sept. 2009. 
1) Numerical and Experimental Observations of Spherical Diffusion Flames. Spherical diffusion flames supported on a
porous sphere were studied numerically and experimentally. Experiments were performed in 2.2 s and 5.2 s microgravity
facilities. Numerical results were obtained from a Chemkin-based program. The program simulates flow from a porous
sphere into a quiescent environment, yields both steady state and transient results and accounts for optically thick
gas-phase radiation. The low flow velocities and long residence times in these diffusion flames lead to enhanced radiative
and diffusive effects. Despite similar adiabatic flame temperatures, the measured and predicted temperatures varied by as
much as 700 K. The temperature reduction correlates with flame size but characteristic flow times and Lewis number
also influence temperature. The numerical results show that the ambient gas Lewis number would have a strong effect
on flame temperature if the flames were steady and nonradiating. For example, a 10% decrease in Lewis number would
increase the steady state flame temperature by 200 K. However, for these transient, radiating flames the effect of Lewis
number is small. It was also observed that when hydrocarbon fuel is supplied from the ambient the large diffusion
distances associated with these flames can lead to unusual steady state compositions near the outer boundary because
decomposition products can diffuse to the outer boundary. This results in a loss of chemical enthalpy from the system
but the effect on flame temperature is small. Transient predictions of flame sizes are larger than those observed in
microgravity experiments. Close agreement could not be obtained without either increasing the model’s thermal and
mass diffusion properties by 30% or reducing mass flowrate by 25%. 
2) Radiative Extinction of Gaseous Spherical Diffusion Flames in Microgravity. Radiative extinction of spherical
diffusion flames was investigated experimentally and numerically. The experiments involved microgravity spherical
diffusion flames burning ethylene and propane at 0.98 bar. Both normal (fuel flowing into oxidizer) and inverse
(oxidizer flowing into fuel) flames were studied, with nitrogen supplied to either the fuel or the oxygen. Flame
conditions were chosen to ensure that the flames extinguished within the 2.2 s of available test time; thus extinction
occurred during unsteady flame conditions. Diagnostics included color video and thin-filament pyrometry. The
computations, which simulated flow from a porous sphere into a quiescent environment, included detailed chemistry,
transport, and radiation and yielded transient results. Radiative extinction was observed experimentally and simulated
numerically. Extinction time, peak temperature, and radiative loss fraction were found to be independent of flow rate
except at very low flow rates. Radiative heat loss was dominated by the combustion products downstream of the flame
and was found to scale with flame surface area, not volume. For large transient flames the heat release rate also scaled
with surface area and thus the radiative loss fraction was largely independent of flow rate. Peak temperatures at
extinction onset were about 1100 K, which is significantly lower than for kinetic extinction. An important observation of
this work is that while radiative heat losses can drive transient extinction, this is not only because radiative losses are
increasing with time but also because the heat release rate is falling off as the flame expands away from the burner and
the reactant supply to the flame decreases. 
3) Soot Inception Limits in Laminar Diffusion Flames with Application to Oxy–Fuel Combustion. For diffusion flames,
the combination of oxygen enrichment and fuel dilution results in an increase in the stoichiometric mixture fraction, Zst,
and alters the flame structure, i.e., the relationship between the local temperature and the local gas composition.
Increasing Zst has been shown to result in the reduction or even elimination of soot. In the present work, the effects of
variable Zst on soot inception are investigated in normal and inverse coflow flames, using ethylene as the fuel. Use of
the inverse coflow flame underscores the validity of these concepts, since the convective field in the inverse flame directs
particles into the fuel-rich region. Sooting limits based on particle luminosity are measured as a function of Zst. The
sooting limit is obtained by varying the amount of inert gas until soot appears above a predefined height. For each limit
flame, the adiabatic flame temperature is calculated and the flame temperature at the half-height is measured. The flame
temperature at the sooting limit is found to increase with Zst for both normal and inverse flames. The effects of residence
time are also investigated, and the sooting limit inception temperature is found to be dependent on fuel stream velocity
for both the normal and inverse configurations. A simple model applicable to oxy–fuel combustion is presented which
describes how increasing Zst results in the reduction and ultimately elimination of soot. This model assumes that soot
inception can only occur in a region where critical values for species, temperature, and residence time are met. The soot
inception region is shown to be bounded by two isotherms: a low-temperature boundary that is a function of residence
time, and a high-temperature boundary that corresponds to the location of a critical local carbon-to-oxygen ratio. The
effect of increasing Zst is to move the boundaries of the soot inception zone towards each other, until the zone is
infinitely thin and thus the sooting limit is reached. By comparing the model to experimental data, a critical local C/O
ratio of 0.53 and a sooting limit inception temperature of 1640 K (for a characteristic residence time of 22 ms) were
determined for ethylene. 
4) Characteristics of Non-Premixed Oxygen-Enhanced Combustion: I. The Presence of Appreciable Oxygen at the
Location of Maximum Temperature. The presence of appreciable molecular oxygen at the location of maximum
temperature has been observed in non-premixed oxygen-enhanced combustion (OEC) processes, specifically in flames
having a high stoichiometric mixture fraction (Zst) produced with diluted fuel and oxygen-enrichment. For conventional
fuel–air flames, key features of the flame are consistent with the flame sheet approximation (FSA). In particular, the

Task Progress:
The following is a summary of the research activities during this period. 
1) Work has continued on radiative extinction of spherical diffusion flames, which are being investigated
experimentally and numerically. The experiments involved microgravity spherical diffusion flames burning ethylene and
propane at 0.98 bar. Both normal (fuel flowing into oxidizer) and inverse (oxidizer flowing into fuel) flames are studied,
with nitrogen supplied to either the fuel or the oxygen. Flame conditions were chosen to ensure that the flames
extinguished within the 2.2 s of available test time; thus extinction occurred during unsteady flame conditions.
Diagnostics included color video and thin-filament pyrometry. The computations, which simulated flow from a porous
sphere into a quiescent environment, include detailed transport properties, detailed chemistry and an optically thick
radiative heat loss model to study the transient radiative extinction of spherical diffusion flames supported by a porous
spherical burner. A flow of a reactant (fuel or oxidizer) issued from a porous sphere into a quiescent environment of the
other reactant was considered in the simulation. Radiative extinction was observed experimentally and simulated
numerically. Extinction time, peak temperature, and radiative loss fraction (ratio of the radiative heat loss rate to the heat
generation rate) were found to be independent of flow rate except at very low flow rates. Radiative heat loss was
dominated by the combustion products downstream of the flame and was found to scale with flame surface area, not
volume. For large transient flames the heat release rate also scaled with surface area and thus the radiative loss fraction
was largely independent of flow rate. Peak temperatures at extinction onset were about 1100 K, which is significantly
lower than for kinetic extinction. One observation is that while radiative heat losses can drive transient extinction, this is
not because radiative losses are increasing with time (flame size) but rather because the heat release rate is falling off as
the temperature drops. 
2) A new numerical study of limiting conditions for soot particle inception in spherical diffusion flames has been
initiated. The flames were modeled using a one-dimensional, time accurate diffusion flame code with detailed chemistry
and transport and an optically thick radiation model. Seventeen normal and inverse flames were considered, covering a
wide range of stoichiometric mixture fraction, adiabatic flame temperature, and residence time. These flames were
previously observed to reach their sooting limits after 2 s of microgravity. Sooting-limit diffusion flames with residence
times longer than 200 ms were found to have temperatures near 1190 K where C/O = 0.6, whereas flames with shorter
residence times required increased temperatures. Acetylene was found to be a reasonable surrogate for soot precursor
species in these flames, having peak mole fractions of about 0.01. 
3) Our previous studies have shown a linear relationship between the limit flame temperature and stoichiometric
mixture fraction for coflow flames at their sooting limit. The sooting limit in coflow flames is obtained by varying the
amount of inert gas until soot appears above a predefined height. In this work sooting limits based on particle luminosity
were measured as a function of Zst. For each limit flame, the flame temperature was measured and defined as the limit
flame temperature. The limit flame temperature increases linearly with Zst, for both normal and inverse flames. The
effects of residence time have also been investigated, and the soot inception temperature was found to be dependent on
fuel stream velocity for both the normal and inverse configuration. Earlier experimental results and modeling indicated
that a critical carbon to oxygen (C/O) ratio exists in the fuel rich region of the flame where soot inception is no longer
favorable due to the presence of oxygen containing species. We extended this model to include the effects of finite rate
chemistry and thus residence time on the sooting limit. This involved considering two boundaries for the soot inception
zone, one dictated by the C/O ratio and the other by the finite rate chemistry of fuel pyrolysis and soot inception. By
combining these two boundaries, a simple model was developed that describes how a sooting limit is attained. By
utilizing measured limit temperature data with the model we found the limit inception temperature for soot formation to
be 1640 K and the critical carbon to oxygen ratio to be 0.53. 
4) Performed analytical analysis of the kinetic and radiative extinction limits of a steady spherical diffusion flame
supported by a porous burner. A single step, overall and irreversible reaction that follows high activation energy
Arrhenius kinetics was adopted to describe the reaction rate. An optically thin radiation model was used to describe the
radiative heat loss. The kinetic and radiative extinction limits were observed from the analysis. Results show that the
flame is easier to extinguish when the radiation intensity is higher, as expected, and there exists a maximum radiation
intensity above which steady burning is not possible.

Task Description:
The overwhelming majority of practical combustion processes involve non-premixed combustion of hydrocarbons.
These processes must be efficient and the resulting emissions low. For example, soot production, which can adversely
affect efficiency, emissions, and equipment lifetime, must be controlled, and flame extinction must be avoided. Many
methods have been considered to reduce soot, such as increasing strain rates and inert concentrations and reducing
pressures, but these techniques generally weaken the flame, leading to extinction and resulting in substantial performance
penalties. The proposed study considers a novel method of approaching combustion to address these concerns. The
approach is termed 'Flame Design' as it attempts to take our fundamental understanding of diffusion flames and, within
the inherent constraints imposed on the problem, design flames that best optimize efficiency and minimize pollutants.
Recognizing the unique benefits of spherical diffusion flames, employ such flames to: 
1. Obtain soot-inception limits as functions of stoichiometric mixture fraction, flow direction (direction across the flame
front, i.e., towards the fuel or towards the oxidizer), amount of inert (nitrogen) and pressure for ethylene and methane
flames. Identify the corresponding soot-inception temperature; 
2. Determine effects of stoichiometric mixture fraction and flow direction on the phenomenon of permanently blue
flames and, to this end, determine the effects of stoichiometric mixture fraction and flow direction on flame temperature,
size, color and soot volume fraction; 
3. Obtain extinction limits as functions of stoichiometric mixture fraction, flow direction, amount of inert and pressure
for ethylene and methane flames. Identify the corresponding extinction temperature; 
4. Evaluate whether this flame system can be used to obtain a pseudo-flammability limit for non-premixed flames in
terms of stoichiometric mixture fraction and amount of inert. If so, determine these limits for ethylene and methane
flames. Soot formation and flame extinction are problems of great concern in practical combustors. Recent
normal-gravity experiments using counterflow diffusion flames have shown that permanently-blue flames (which are
devoid of soot regardless of strain rate) and extinction-resistant flames can be produced for many hydrocarbon fuels by
nitrogen exchange (increased stoichiometric mixture fraction). Furthermore, there is a unique stoichiometric mixture
fraction wherein the flame temperature at extinction can be up to 200 K lower and the strain rate up to four times greater
than that for the standard hydrocarbon/air flame with the same flame temperature. The present experiments employ
microgravity conditions to allow for a unique set of experiments to clarify the underlying mechanisms responsible for
flame response to stoichiometric mixture fraction.

As the goal of this program is to develop an understanding of combustion with the aim of reducing pollutants and
increasing combustion efficiency, the results are directly applicable to terrestial power generation. In particular, with the
present interest in carbon sequestration via oxygen-enriched combustion the present work is of particular importance as
it identifies methods of controlling a number of pollutants through oxygen-enriched combustion.

Below is a summary of the progress on this project for the year ending January 1, 2006: 
Flame Design benefited from several analytical and computational advances in 2005. These have yielded important
insight into many experimental observations by this project. This has laid the groundwork for two journal publications,
one which was submitted and one which is nearing submission. 
Work on the general behavior of spherical diffusion flames has demonstrated that a detailed transient numerical model
(with detailed chemistry, transport, and radiation) can predict flame size and flame temperature for microgravity
spherical diffusion flames. A simplified model indicates that Lewis number can have a large effect on flame temperature
for microgravity diffusion flames. This work has been submitted to the Combustion Symposium. 
Work on radiative extinction of spherical diffusion flames has demonstrated that the detailed transient model can predict
extinction times for microgravity spherical diffusion flames. The work identified several scaling relationship for
extinguishing diffusion flames. This work is being finalized for an upcoming journal submission. 
A Nikon D100 color digital still camera has been configured for use in the 2.2 second drop tower. This provides four
images per drop. The images have 6 megapixels and 12 x 3 bit depth and are among the highest-fidelity images ever
recorded in the drop tower. 
This camera has been used to record images of 15 micron SiC fibers in flames in the 2.2 second drop tower. This has
yielded qualitative thin-filament pyrometry results for extinguishing spherical diffusion flames. Eighteen successful drop
tests were performed in the 2.2 second drop tower.

Task Description:
The overwhelming majority of practical combustion processes involve non-premixed combustion of hydrocarbons.
These processes must be efficient and the resulting emissions low. For example, soot production, which can adversely
affect efficiency, emissions, and equipment lifetime, must be controlled, and flame extinction must be avoided. Many
methods have been considered to reduce soot, such as increasing strain rates and inert concentrations and reducing
pressures, but these techniques generally weaken the flame, leading to extinction and resulting in substantial
performance penalties. The proposed study considers a novel method of approaching combustion to address these
concerns. The approach is termed 'Flame Design' as it attempts to take our fundamental understanding of diffusion flames
and, within the inherent constraints imposed on the problem, design flames that best optimize efficiency and minimize
pollutants. Recognizing the unique benefits of spherical diffusion flames, employ such flames to: 
1. Obtain soot-inception limits as functions of stoichiometric mixture fraction, flow direction (direction across the flame
front, i.e., towards the fuel or towards the oxidizer), amount of inert (nitrogen) and pressure for ethylene and methane
flames. Identify the corresponding soot-inception temperature; 
2. Determine effects of stoichiometric mixture fraction and flow direction on the phenomenon of permanently blue
flames and, to this end, determine the effects of stoichiometric mixture fraction and flow direction on flame
temperature, size, color and soot volume fraction; 
3. Obtain extinction limits as functions of stoichiometric mixture fraction, flow direction, amount of inert and pressure
for ethylene and methane flames. Identify the corresponding extinction temperature; 
4. Evaluate whether this flame system can be used to obtain a pseudo-flammability limit for non-premixed flames in
terms of stoichiometric mixture fraction and amount of inert. If so, determine these limits for ethylene and methane
flames. Soot formation and flame extinction are problems of great concern in practical combustors. Recent
normal-gravity experiments using counterflow diffusion flames have shown that permanently-blue flames (which are
devoid of soot regardless of strain rate) and extinction-resistant flames can be produced for many hydrocarbon fuels by
nitrogen exchange (increased stoichiometric mixture fraction). Furthermore, there is a unique stoichiometric mixture
fraction wherein the flame temperature at extinction can be up to 200 K lower and the strain rate up to four times greater
than that for the standard hydrocarbon/air flame with the same flame temperature. The present experiments employ
microgravity conditions to allow for a unique set of experiments to clarify the underlying mechanisms responsible for
flame response to stoichiometric mixture fraction. 
  
Rationale for HRP Directed Research:  
Research Impact/Earth Benefits:
As the goal of this program is to develop an understanding of combustion with the aim of reducing pollutants and
increasing combustion efficiency, the results are directly applicable to terrestial power generation. In particular, with the
present interest in carbon sequestration via oxygen-enriched combustion the present work is of particular importance as
it identifies methods of controlling a number of pollutants through oxygen-enriched combustion. 
  
Task Progress:
Testing in the 2.2-second drop tower has yielded sooting limits of spherical microgravity diffusion flames reacting
ethylene and oxygen at various levels of dilution. The results provide a quantitative comparison of the effects on
sooting limits of stoichiometric mixture fraction and convection direction. Testing in the drop tower also has identified
a number of extinction limits. 
Normal-gravity tests involving coflowing gas jet flames have considered a similar range of stoichiometric mixture
fraction for both normal and inverse conditions. These results lend support to the microgravity observations. 
Analytical modeling has advanced the understanding of the steady-state temperature, size, and residence times of the
present spherical flames. 
Numerical modeling, which incorporates detailed chemistry and transport, has lent insight into the effects of radiation
and unsteadiness of the present spherical flames. 
These results have been and are being disseminated in the publications listed in the publication section.
